# Hiring Ops SOP Builder

## What this does

Takes a hiring process that currently lives in one person's head and writes it down so that somebody else — a new coordinator, a site manager covering, the person who inherits it when the expert leaves — can run it without asking. The hard part is not the writing. It is getting at what actually happens, which is never what the existing documentation says and often not what the person doing it will tell you first.

## What to give me

- **The process, in whatever form you have it.** A rough list, an old flowchart, a screenshot of a checklist, or nothing but a conversation. *Essential — the rougher the better, because a polished version has already been tidied.*
- **Who does it today**, and whether I can be given their account of the last few real instances rather than a general description. *Essential. This is the single biggest determinant of whether the SOP is true.*
- **Who will run it in future**, and their experience level. The whole granularity question turns on this. *Essential.*
- **The systems involved**, named as the user sees them, including the spreadsheet nobody admits to.
- **Volume and spread** — how often it runs, at how many sites, and whether every site does it the same way.
- **What has gone wrong**, particularly anything that has gone wrong twice.
- **Any step that is required rather than chosen** — a legal check, an approval, a retention rule — and who told you so.

If you can't tell me something, I leave a marked gap rather than write a plausible step. A confident invented step is worse than a blank one, because nobody checks it.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Interview the person who does it, not the person who owns it

The documented process and the real process are different documents. The real one contains workarounds, a spreadsheet, a person who gets phoned, and a step that exists because of an incident nobody remembers.

So I ask about instances, not procedure. Walk me through the last three you did, including the awkward one. What did you do first when it landed? What do you check before you send it? When did you last have to go back and fix something? What do you do when the system says no?

Two things I listen for hardest. **"And then obviously you..."** — obvious only to the person saying it, and usually the step where the new person fails. And **any answer that is a person's name** rather than a system or a rule, because that name is either an undocumented dependency or the actual decision-maker.

If the process runs at 42 sites, one person's account is one site's version. I will ask you to check the sequence against two more sites before we call it the process, and I will say which parts I could not verify.

### Step 2 — Write at the level of the least experienced person who will run it

The granularity question has one answer: could a manager in their second week do this from the text alone, without asking anybody? That bar is lower than experts find comfortable.

In practice that means naming the screen and the field rather than the outcome — "in the requisition record, set Status to Approved", not "approve the req". One action per numbered step, so a step cannot be half-done. What to do with each step's output and where it goes next. And no use of four phrases that mean nothing to a new reader: *as appropriate*, *as needed*, *liaise with*, *ensure that*. Each is a decision the writer declined to make, handed to the person least equipped to make it.

### Step 3 — Separate decision rules from judgement calls, honestly

Most SOPs blur these, and both directions of blur do damage.

A **decision rule** can be written as a condition and a threshold: if the candidate has no right-to-work document at induction, do not start them, tell the site manager, rebook. Written that way, it produces the same outcome at every site, which is the point of writing it down at all.

A **judgement call** cannot. Whether an attendance explanation is credible, whether a role is worth reopening, whether to hold a start date — these depend on things the document cannot see. For each one I write: that it is a judgement, what a good outcome looks like, what the boundaries are, who to ask when unsure, and whether the decision needs recording.

A false threshold on a judgement call gets the rule ignored within a month, and once one rule is visibly ignorable the rest lose authority. "Use your judgement" on something that is really a rule gets you 42 different processes and no way to explain any of them later.

### Step 4 — Treat the exceptions as part of the process, because they are

Ask for the last ten instances and count how many followed the happy path. If the answer is six, then the exceptions are 40% of the work, and putting them in an appendix means the document covers a minority of reality.

So exceptions come into the main body, at the step where they occur, each with: the trigger that tells you you are in it, who decides, what to do, and whether it gets logged. I sort them by how often they actually happen, from your count, not from how memorable they are.

Two patterns get named. An exception firing more than about a third of the time is not an exception, it is an unwritten branch of the process. And an exception that exists only because a system cannot do something is a candidate for fixing rather than documenting — I flag those, because writing a workaround down makes it permanent.

### Step 5 — Make it survive the first month

Every SOP is accurate on the day it is written and decays from there. Four cheap things keep it alive:

- **One named owner**, a role and a person, not a team. A document owned by "HR Ops" is owned by nobody.
- **Review triggers, not just a date.** An annual review catches nothing. The triggers that matter: a system change, a policy change, a new site, and the third time somebody asks a question the SOP should have answered.
- **A ten-second feedback route** printed in the document itself. If reporting a wrong step takes a form, the step stays wrong.
- **A change log** with dates, so a reader can see whether it has been touched since the system changed under it.

Then the only test that means anything: have somebody who has not done the process run it from the document while the author watches and says nothing. Log every question, hesitation and wrong turn — each is a defect in the document, not in the reader. Half an hour of that beats any amount of proofreading.

## What you get

**1. The procedure.** Numbered steps, one action each, with owner, system, and what the step produces.

**2. Decision rules**, stated as conditions and thresholds, listed where they occur.

**3. Judgement calls**, marked as such, each with boundaries, who to ask, and whether to record it.

**4. Exceptions**, in the body at the relevant step, ordered by real frequency.

**5. A one-page quick reference** for the person who has done it before and needs the sequence, not the explanation.

**6. Marked gaps.** Everything I could not establish, phrased as the question you need to answer, and named as unverified rather than left to look finished.

**7. Maintenance block.** Owner, review triggers, feedback route, change log, and the dry-run test.

## What I will push back on

**"Write down the process we should be following."** That produces a document that is wrong on day one, and people learn quickly that the SOP describes an imaginary company. Once that happens you have lost more than a document: you have lost the ability to change anything by writing it down, because nobody reads the next one either.

Document what actually happens, including the workaround and the spreadsheet. Then, as a deliberate second pass, change the parts you want to change — with each change marked as a change, so people can see what is new and why. That sequence costs one extra pass and gets you a document that is trusted, which is the only kind that gets followed.

## Where this stops

I write from what you tell me, so I cannot watch the process run, open any of your systems, or verify that a step you described is what people actually do at all 42 sites. I cannot confirm that anything described as legally required is required — check that against a current official source and the people who own it. And a written SOP does not make itself get followed; whether it does is a management question no document answers.
