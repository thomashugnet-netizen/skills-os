## What this does

Frontline employers complete enormous numbers of I-9s, often at sites, often by managers who do it a few times a month. That combination — high volume, distributed completion, untrained completers — is exactly where the common errors come from. This helps you build a process that produces fewer of them, and know where to look for the ones you already have.

## What to give me

- **How you currently do it**: paper or electronic, completed at site or centrally, who fills in Section 2, whether you use E-Verify.
- **Your volume and pattern** — steady, or seasonal spikes where hundreds of people start in a fortnight.
- **How many sites, and how many people are authorised to complete the employer section.** Add how many of them changed in the last year if you know. Exposure scales with completers, not with hires, and these are the numbers the workload section is built from.
- **Which states you operate in.** Some states mandate E-Verify for some employers; some restrict aspects of verification practice.
- **What you're worried about**, or what prompted this. Preparing for an audit, a recent acquisition with inherited records, and building a new process are three different jobs.

## Before you paste anything

Do not paste completed forms, document numbers, identity document images, dates of birth, social security numbers, or anything that identifies an employee. There is no version of this analysis that needs them, and I will stop and ask you to describe the situation instead.

If you want to check aggregate completion status, send counts only — for example: completions by site, count of Section 2s completed outside three business days, count of records missing a signature. Categorical and numeric, no individuals.

Completed I-9s are sensitive records with their own storage and access requirements. Keep them in the system that holds them.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Map your current process against where errors originate

The common failures are structural rather than individual, and they concentrate in a few places:

**Section 1 (the employee's part).** Completed late or incompletely; missing signature or date; the attestation box left blank or a second box also ticked; a preparer/translator used but that certification left unsigned.

**Section 2 (the employer's part).** This is where most volume employers accumulate the most exposure:
- Completed outside the required window after the first day of work.
- Over-documentation — asking for more documents than required, or specifying which ones. This is not merely an error; it can constitute unlawful discrimination.
- Under-documentation — accepting an incomplete List A, or one List B/C document where two are needed.
- The employer representative's own details or signature missing.
- The first-day-of-employment date left blank or wrong.

**Reverification and retention.** Missed reverification dates; records destroyed too early, or kept too long without a defensible policy; terminated-employee records retained on the wrong clock.

**Distribution.** The same error repeating at one site because one manager was taught wrong once. This is the single highest-yield thing to look for in a multi-site employer, and it is invisible in an aggregate error rate.

### Step 2 — Find the structural fix, not the individual correction

For each error class I distinguish what has to be corrected on a record from what has to change in the process so it stops recurring. Fixing three hundred forms and leaving the process alone means you will fix three hundred more next year.

Where a correction is needed, I will describe the *kind* of correction generally understood to be appropriate — and tell you to verify the current method against the official handbook, because correction practice has specifics (who may correct, how to annotate, what must never be erased or backdated) that matter and that change.

### Step 3 — Build the process for your volume

High volume changes the right answer. What works at twenty hires a month fails at four hundred. I'll design for:

- **Where completion happens** and who is authorised to complete Section 2, which is a real decision with real consequences at scale.
- **The timing trigger** — the clock runs from the first day of work, so the process has to be driven by actual start dates, including when a start moves.
- **Who trains the completers**, and how you keep that consistent across sites and through manager turnover, which in frontline operations is fast.
- **The seasonal spike**, if you have one. Peak hiring is when error rates rise, because the same people complete far more forms under time pressure.
- **Self-audit cadence**, sampled rather than exhaustive, so it actually happens.

### Step 4 — E-Verify, if you use it

Enrolment obligations, the timing relationship to the I-9, what a tentative non-confirmation requires of you and of the employee, the notice and referral steps, and what you must not do while a case is open — most significantly, the constraints on adverse action during that period. I'll map your process against those steps and flag where yours diverges. Again: verify against e-verify.gov, and note that some state requirements sit on top of the federal ones.

## What you get

**1. Process map** — your current flow, with the error-prone points marked.

**2. Error-class checklist** — what to look for, in likelihood order for an employer of your shape.

**3. Structural fixes** — process changes, distinguished from record corrections, each with an owner.

**4. Completion checklist for managers** — one page, written out in full and in plain language, for the person actually filling in Section 2, one card per completion route you run, not a description of what a card should contain. Usually the highest-value output here, because most errors are made by someone who was never given one.

**5. Self-audit plan** — the check sheet itself, a line per item, plus sample size, how it is drawn across sites, cadence, and what happens to a finding.

**6. Training and re-training plan** — who briefs a new completer, in what form, how soon after they take the role, and how you know it happened.

**7. Verification list** — everything in the output that you must confirm against the current official source before relying on it. I'd rather hand you a short list of things to check than let you assume none of it moved.

## What it costs you to run

Counted in records and people, not in risk. Take 40 sites, 300 hires a month — 3,600 records a year — and two authorised completers per site.

**Self-audit.** A ten percent sample is 360 records a year. Each record is checked against roughly a dozen things: every field on both sections present, signatures and dates where they belong, the completion date against the required window measured from the actual first day of work, reverification dates where they apply. That is about 4,300 individual checks a year. At four minutes a record that is around 24 hours, and it is the sampled version; an inherited back catalogue after an acquisition is the full count.

**Reconciling the dates.** The clock runs from the actual first day of work rather than the scheduled one, so each of those 300 records a month has to be read against what the rota says happened, not what the offer said. Starts move, and nobody amends the record when they do.

**Training the completers.** Two per site is about 80 people to train, each needing the card and a session with somebody who can answer a question. Frontline manager turnover being what it is, a third changing in a year is around 27 re-trainings, and each of those is a person completing their first employer section on a real hire, unsupervised, in their first weeks in the role.

**Corrections.** Apply whatever defect rate your own sample shows to 3,600 records. One in twenty is 180 records to put right, and a correction is an individual act on an individual record, done by a specific person in a specific way. None of it is bulk work.

**The distribution problem.** One site taught wrong once produces around 90 records a year at that site alone, and a sample spread evenly across 40 sites may catch one of them. Finding it means auditing by site rather than by total: 40 small audits instead of one.
