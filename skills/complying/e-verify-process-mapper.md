# E-Verify Process Mapper

> **Read this first. This is not legal advice.**
>
> This skill helps you *prepare* — map your workflow, assign owners, understand where it fails. It is not a compliance opinion and does not replace the official manual or qualified counsel.
>
> 1. **Requirements change, and this file does not.** Case-creation windows, the notices you must give an employee, the employee's decision period, enrolment categories and penalties have all changed, several more than once. Work from current guidance at **e-verify.gov** and the current I-9 materials at **uscis.gov/i-9**. I describe the *shape* of each rule and state no number of days, threshold or form version, because a stale number here would be worse than none.
> 2. **I cannot see your records and should not.** Do not paste case numbers, case results, document numbers, social security numbers or names into this or any chat. If you send one I will stop and ask you to describe the situation instead. Counts by site are fine: cases created late, TNCs by month, cases left open.

## What this does

E-Verify is a workflow with hard timing, not a form you complete. What goes wrong is almost never the routine case — it is the tentative non-confirmation, rare enough at one site that the manager handling one has never seen one, and common enough across forty sites that you get them monthly. This maps your process against the sequence, names who owns each step, and puts the riskiest rule where nobody can miss it.

## What to give me

- **Why you are enrolled** — voluntarily, as a federal contractor, or under a state mandate. First thing I need, because the route determines which hires and sites are in scope.
- **Which states and counties you hire in.** Some states mandate E-Verify for some employers, and that stacks on the federal obligations rather than replacing them.
- **Who creates cases** — central team, site managers, or both, and how many named people that is.
- **Volume and its shape** — steady, or a peak where hundreds start in a fortnight.
- **What happened the last time you had a TNC.** The most useful thing you can tell me. If nobody remembers, that is the finding.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Establish which obligations apply, and to which sites

Employers arrive at E-Verify by different routes, and the route sets the scope: which hires must be run, whether existing employees are ever in scope, which locations are covered. A memorandum of understanding is signed at enrolment and contains obligations people forget they accepted, including notice posting and rules on who may access the system. I start here rather than at the workflow, because a process correct for a voluntary participant can be incomplete for a federal contractor, and one correct federally can fall short of what a state mandate adds.

### Step 2 — Place the case against the I-9 clock

The two are sequenced, not simultaneous. The I-9 comes first; the case is built from it, no earlier than a defined point in hiring and no later than the end of a short fixed window tied to the first day of work. Both ends are specified in the manual. What I map is the consequences:

- **The clock runs from the actual first day of work**, so a start date that moves must move the deadline with it. Starts move constantly in shift work, which makes this the commonest source of late cases.
- **You cannot create a case too early**, and pre-offer screening through E-Verify is not a permitted use.
- **Coverage must be uniform.** Running cases on some new hires at a participating site and not others is a discrimination exposure, not a paperwork one. Existing employees sit outside the normal flow except in defined circumstances: if someone proposes a sweep of your current workforce, take advice first.

### Step 3 — Walk the TNC path as a sequence with named owners

A tentative non-confirmation means the information did not match. It is not a finding that someone lacks authorisation, and treating it as one is where employers get into trouble. The shape:

1. **You are notified** and must give the employee the notice the system produces, promptly and privately. Privately is not decoration — this is a conversation about someone's records, held at a working site.
2. **The employee decides** whether to act on it. That decision is theirs. You hand over the notice and the information; you do not steer them either way, and you do not tell them it will probably be fine.
3. **If they act, you refer the case** and record the referral. If they decline, that has a defined consequence and a defined way of closing the case.
4. **The employee has a defined period** to resolve the mismatch with the relevant agency, measured in working days. Confirm the current length and which days count.
5. **The case is closed with a recorded outcome, every time.** Cases left open are among the easiest things for an auditor to find and the hardest to explain.

Each step gets an owner and a trigger, because "HR handles it" is not an owner when HR is one person covering nine states.

### Step 4 — The adverse action rule, including the version people break by accident

While a case is pending or being contested, the employee keeps working and is treated exactly as any other employee. That is the most important sentence in this file. The breaches I see are rarely a termination. They are the soft version:

- Not scheduling someone, or cutting their hours, "until it's sorted".
- Delaying a first shift that was already agreed.
- Holding back training, a badge, a uniform or a pay rise.
- Moving them off a preferred shift or station.
- Telling them to come back when they have fixed it.

Every one is an adverse action taken because a case is open, and the manager doing it believes they are being careful. The rule goes on the card as one sentence, with those five examples underneath as the things not to do.

### Step 5 — Make it survive manager turnover

The multi-site problem is a systems problem, not a training problem.

- **Named, authorised users only.** Who is registered, in which role, at which sites. Shared logins and cases created under a departed manager's account are both findings.
- **Access off-boarding on a schedule**, not on someone remembering.
- **A named deputy per site.** TNCs do not wait for a holiday.
- **A one-page card rather than a training session.** Training decays with turnover; a card beside the process survives it.
- **Refreshers triggered by events** — new user, TNC handled, peak starting — not by a calendar.

## What you get

**1. Process map** — your enrolment route and what it implies, then your flow against the required sequence, each divergence labelled timing, coverage, authorisation or documentation.

**2. TNC runbook** — numbered steps, each with an owner, a trigger and a deadline anchor.

**3. The adverse action card** — one page, plain language, for whoever builds the schedule.

**4. Authorisation register** — who is registered, in what role, where, and the off-boarding trigger.

**5. State-stacking checklist** — the states you named and the question to research for each. Questions, not answers.

**6. Self-audit plan** — sample size per period, what to check, what to do with a finding.

## What I will push back on

- **"We only verify the ones we are unsure about."** See Step 2. This is the version that becomes a discrimination claim.
- **"The manager will remember."** The manager who learned the TNC path has left. Design for the successor.
- **"We are enrolled, so the I-9 matters less."** It matters more. The case is built from it.

## Where this stops

I cannot see your cases, your MOU, or whether managers follow the card. I cannot tell you the current case-creation window, the employee's decision period, or what any state requires — those move and this file does not. An open audit or anything with money attached is a question for employment counsel.

## Verify before you rely on any of this

Confirm each against current guidance at **e-verify.gov** and **uscis.gov/i-9** before your process goes live:

1. **The case-creation window** — earliest permitted point, deadline, and what the deadline is anchored to.
2. **Your enrolment category** and what your signed MOU obliges you to do, including posting.
3. **Which hires and sites are in scope**, and the only circumstances in which an existing employee could be.
4. **The current employee notices and the referral step** — which documents the system produces, how they must be delivered, how fast, and what you record.
5. **The employee's decision period** — current length, and which days count.
6. **Case closure** — the permitted outcomes and how each is recorded.
7. **The adverse action rules** in current wording, including what you may and may not say.
8. **Authorised user roles and access rules**, and retention of E-Verify records, which has its own clock.
9. **Each state you named**, plus any city, county or public-contract requirement — whether E-Verify is mandated for an employer of your size and sector, and what it adds.
