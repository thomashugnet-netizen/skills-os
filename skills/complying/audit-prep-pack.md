# Audit Prep Pack

> **Read this first. This is not legal advice.**
>
> This skill helps you *prepare* — build the inventory, design a self-audit, decide who speaks. It is not a compliance opinion and does not replace counsel.
>
> 1. **Requirements change, and this file does not.** Retention periods, notice periods, what an agency may demand and in what format, penalty structures and the rules on electronic records have all changed. Work from current guidance at **uscis.gov/i-9**, **e-verify.gov** and **dol.gov**, plus your state labour department. I describe the *shape* of what an audit asks for and state no deadline, retention period, penalty figure or document list, because a stale specific would be worse than nothing.
> 2. **I cannot see your records and should not.** Do not paste I-9s, timesheets, payroll registers, personnel files or any employee identifier into this or any chat. If you send one I will stop and ask you to describe the situation instead. Counts are fine: records by site, records missing a signature, weeks with a missed break.
>
> **If you have an active audit, a notice of inspection, or anything with money attached, call counsel now.** Prep is for before. This helps you arrive organised, not respond.

## What this does

Most audit damage is not caused by the underlying practice. It is caused by not being able to produce, inside a short window, records you genuinely have, in a form somebody else can read. This builds that inventory in advance and separates the two kinds of finding an audit produces.

## What to give me

- **Which kind you are preparing for** — right-to-work, wage and hour, or both. One does not prepare you for the other.
- **Where records live**, and in how many systems. Paper at site, HRIS, payroll provider, time clock, a spreadsheet a regional manager keeps. A longer list than people expect.
- **How many sites and states**, and whether record practice varies between them, and **any acquisition in the last few years** — see Step 5.
- **Who would field the first call.** If that is unclear, it is finding number one.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Map what an audit asks for, in shape

An inspection is a request for a defined set of things inside a short window — short enough that assembling from scratch is not realistic. Confirm the current notice period and permitted formats from the source, not from here.

**Right-to-work inspections** center on completed forms for a defined population over a defined period, the supporting records you chose to retain, and evidence of your process. Where the system is electronic, they also ask it to prove it does what a paper record would: audit trails, indexing, integrity. Otherwise clean employers fail on those alone.

**Wage-hour inspections** center on time records, pay records and the relationship between them: hours, rate, overtime calculation, deductions, break records where a state requires them, and the classification decisions behind all of it. Job descriptions and schedules come too, because that is where classification is evidenced.

Both types also ask for policies and for evidence the policy was operated. A policy nobody followed is worse than none: it establishes what you knew you should be doing.

### Step 2 — Build the inventory before you need it

One document. Per record type: what it is, where it lives, which system is authoritative when two disagree, who can produce it, in what format, how long that takes, and what its retention clock is anchored to.

That last field is where the errors sit. Record types run on different clocks — from hire, from separation, from the pay period — and when more than one applies, the longer governs. Two things I look for:

- **The single point of failure.** One person who knows how to pull the time export. One regional manager with a laptop folder. One vendor contract that expires before your retention period does.
- **The orphaned record.** Anything held by a former provider, a decommissioned system, a closed site, a departed manager. This produces "we have it, we just cannot get it", which reads identically to not having it.

### Step 3 — Design the self-audit sample so it happens

Exhaustive review does not happen twice. A sample does.

- **Stratify by site**, not by date. The commonest real finding is one site with a systematic error, because one manager was taught wrong once, and a whole-population sample averages that away.
- **Include the edge cases deliberately**: rehires, interstate transfers, minors, seasonal starts, anyone whose start date moved, anyone whose reverification date has passed.
- **Fix sample size, stratification and pass criteria before you look**, and keep them constant each cycle. Otherwise the exercise concludes things are broadly fine, and your rate moves for methodological reasons rather than real ones.
- **Settle privilege first.** A self-audit creates a written record of your own problems. Take advice on how it is conducted and held before you start.

### Step 4 — Separate the record problem from the process problem

Every finding is one of two things, and conflating them is why employers audit twice and fix nothing.

A **record problem** is a defect on one document: a missing signature, a blank field, a record filed in the wrong place. The fix is a correction, done however current guidance permits — confirm that method, because who may correct, how it is annotated and what must never be erased or backdated all have specifics and all have moved.

A **process problem** is the reason that defect exists. It shows up as the same error clustering by site, manager, month or role. The fix is upstream: who does the task, what they were given, what triggers it, what catches it.

So I sort findings by cluster before severity. Three hundred corrections with the process untouched buys three hundred more next year. Where a cluster implies a practice rather than an error, stop and take advice.

### Step 5 — Chain of custody, one voice, and inherited records

**Who speaks.** One named person fields the auditor, with one named deputy. Site managers do not answer substantive questions, hand over records, or explain the process. This is not evasiveness; an incorrect description of your own process becomes part of the record. Managers get a card: be courteous, take a name, call this number.

**Chain of custody.** Log every record produced: what, when, to whom, in what format, and what copy you kept.

**Inherited records.** An acquisition means records created under a process you never controlled, by people you cannot ask, possibly in a system you no longer run. They are your exposure now. Inventory and sample them separately, and expect a different error profile. The common gap: the population itself is uncertain, so you cannot show a record is missing rather than never created. Reconstructing the roster from payroll history is the first job, and worth doing before anyone asks for it.

## What you get

**1. Record inventory template** — type, location, system of record, owner, format, production time, retention anchor, and the request it answers.

**2. Gap list, ranked** — anything with no owner, no clock, or a production time longer than a plausible window.

**3. Self-audit design** — sample size, stratification, edge cases, pass criteria, cycle, privilege.

**4. Finding triage rule** — how to sort a finding into record or process, and the cluster test that separates them.

**5. Response protocol** — who speaks, who deputises, the manager card, the custody log.

**6. What I could not determine** — anything I assumed about your systems, listed rather than buried in an inventory that looks complete.

## What I will push back on

- **"We will pull it together if it happens."** Short window, six systems. That is the plan that fails.
- **"Our error rate is 2%."** Across sites that can mean one at 40% and thirty at zero. Ask for the per-site number.

## Where this stops

I cannot see your records, count your gaps, or tell you whether anything in your file room is a violation. I do not know the current retention periods, notice periods or penalty structures, and will not guess. The audit itself belongs with counsel.

## Verify before you rely on any of this

Confirm each against a current official source — **uscis.gov/i-9**, **e-verify.gov**, **dol.gov**, your state labour department:

1. **Retention periods** per record type, and exactly what each clock is anchored to.
2. **Notice periods** for the inspections you are preparing for, and what may be demanded without notice.
3. **Permitted formats**, and the rules for electronic storage, audit trails and indexing.
4. **The correction method** per record type: who may correct, how it is annotated, what must never be altered.
5. **Time, pay, break and meal record content** required federally and per state, plus state inspection rights.
6. **How a self-audit should be conducted and held**, with counsel, before you run one.
7. **Successor liability** for inherited records, with counsel, if you have acquired anything.
