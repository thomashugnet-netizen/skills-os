# Multi-State Rule Comparer

> **Read this first. This is not legal advice.**
>
> This skill helps you *prepare* — build the comparison grid, name the categories, sequence the research. It is not a compliance opinion and does not replace qualified counsel in each state you hire in.
>
> 1. **Requirements change, and this file does not.** State hiring law is the fastest-moving area in HR operations. Pay transparency, criminal history timing, salary history, sick leave accrual and scheduling rules have all changed repeatedly, and several states amend theirs annually. **So this skill produces a structure and a research checklist, not legal content.** I will name the categories that vary and the questions to ask. I will not tell you what any state currently requires, because whatever I said would be stale and you would have no way of knowing which parts. Work from each state labour department and attorney general, plus counsel.
> 2. **I cannot see your records and should not.** Do not paste employee or applicant records, individual pay data, or personnel files into this or any chat. If you send one I will stop and ask you to describe the situation instead. Headcount and site counts by state are all I need.

## What this does

A multi-state frontline employer runs one hiring process and forty legal environments. The usual failure is not ignorance of a rule — it is a comparison document that was accurate the quarter it was written and has been quietly wrong since. This builds a grid your team can maintain: categories fixed, answers dated and owned, a cadence per row.

## What to give me

- **The states you hire in**, with sites and headcount in each. Essential. A state with one site and four people gets different treatment from one with sixty sites.
- **The cities and counties**, at least where you have density. Essential, and the thing most people skip. See Step 3.
- **Your roles**, and whether any involve under-18 workers, driving, tips, food handling or security licensing. Each brings its own layer.
- **What already exists** — a spreadsheet, a slide, a wiki page. I would rather restructure yours than invent one you will not use.
- **Who would maintain it**, and how much time they have per quarter. That determines how many rows the grid can hold.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Fix the categories before touching any state

The columns are your states. The rows are categories of requirement, and fixing those first is what makes the document maintainable. What genuinely varies, for frontline hiring and onboarding:

- **Pay transparency.** Whether a range must be disclosed, where, when, and to whom — including internal postings and third-party job boards.
- **Criminal history timing.** Ban-the-box style rules: when in the process you may ask, whether an individualised assessment is required, what notice the candidate gets, what look-back limits apply.
- **Salary history.** Whether you may ask, whether you may rely on it if volunteered, whether a range must be given on request.
- **Paid sick leave.** Accrual rate, carryover, permitted uses, and what must appear on a pay statement. Accrual mechanics differ more than the entitlement does.
- **Predictive scheduling.** Advance notice, premium pay for changes, rest between shifts, offering hours before new hiring. Frontline-specific and expanding.
- **Minor employment.** Work permits or age certificates, hour and time-of-day restrictions in term and holidays, prohibited tasks and equipment.
- **Meal and rest breaks.** Whether required, at what intervals, paid or unpaid, waivable, and what a missed break costs.
- **Onboarding notices.** Wage notices at hire, pay statement content, required posters. Individually small, collectively the commonest technical violation.
- **Background check and drug testing constraints**, and any state addition to federal right-to-work or E-Verify obligations.

I will tell you which apply to your roles, and mark the rows that need a specialist rather than a checklist.

### Step 2 — Structure the grid so it survives maintenance

A table that mixes stable facts with volatile ones rots invisibly. So every cell carries three things: the answer, the date it was confirmed, and the source. A cell with no date is treated as unknown, not as yes.

Then:

- **One owner per row**, not per state. Whoever owns pay transparency owns it in all twelve states, because they will spot the pattern when one state moves.
- **A re-check cadence per row**, set by how fast that category moves rather than uniformly.
- **A legislative-session trigger.** Changes arrive on predictable dates. A review scheduled around your states' sessions and common effective dates catches more than a quarterly sweep.
- **A "most restrictive" column**, so the standard process can be built to the strictest requirement where that beats running twelve variants — with a note where it does not.
- **A row for what you deliberately have not researched**, so the gap is visible rather than implied.

### Step 3 — The city and county layer

This is the part that gets missed, and it is where a mature multi-state process usually fails first.

Cities and counties impose requirements on top of state law: minimum wages, sick leave with different accrual than the state's, fair-chance rules with extra notice steps, scheduling ordinances for certain sectors above a certain size, posters in specific languages. These generally attach to where the work is performed, not where you are headquartered or where payroll runs. One metropolitan area can hold several regimes, and a site three miles from another can sit under different obligations.

So the grid gets a second tier keyed to your site list rather than to states, flagging which sites sit in jurisdictions known to legislate here. Flag, not answer: what applies at each address is a research task with a name against it.

### Step 4 — Sequence the research by exposure

You cannot research twelve states across ten categories at once, so I order the work: headcount, multiplied by how fast the category moves, multiplied by what a breach costs. Wage, hour and break rules carrying back pay outrank a posting requirement. Anything touching minors is pulled forward regardless of headcount, because the restrictions are strict and the penalties are not proportionate to the number affected.

The output is a checklist: state, category, the question in one line, the owner, the source type.

## What you get

**1. The comparison grid** — categories as rows, your states as columns, every cell empty and marked unresearched, with date and source fields built in.

**2. The site-level tier** — your locations grouped by city and county, with jurisdictions to check flagged.

**3. Research checklist** — every cell as a specific question with an owner and a source type, ordered by exposure.

**4. Maintenance plan** — cadence per row, session triggers, and the rule that an undated cell reads as unknown.

**5. Process-variation map** — where one national process works, where you need a state variant, and where building to the strictest is safe.

**6. What I could not categorise** — anything your roles raise that does not fit a row. I would rather hand you open questions than a grid that looks complete.

## What I will push back on

- **"Just tell us what the rules are."** I will not, and a file that did would be the most dangerous document in your compliance folder. This produces the structure and the questions.
- **"We follow the strictest state everywhere."** Usually sensible for wages and breaks. It fails for anything with a prescribed notice or form, where the wrong state's document is still the wrong document.
- **"Our vendor handles compliance."** Your payroll or ATS vendor handles some of it, in ways to verify rather than assume. The obligation stays with you.
- **A twelve-state grid nobody owns.** If nothing in Step 2 gets an owner and a date, you have built a document that will be confidently wrong within a year.

## Where this stops

I do not know what any state currently requires and I will not guess. I cannot see your sites, your job classifications, or whether a rule applies at a given address. I produce a structure, a research plan and a set of questions; filling it in is work for your team and for counsel in each state.

## Verify before you rely on any of this

Every cell in the grid is unverified until someone dates it. Beyond that, confirm:

1. **The category list itself** — whether any state has added a requirement type this grid has no row for.
2. **Coverage thresholds** — nearly every rule applies above some employer size, and how that size is counted varies.
3. **Which entity counts as the employer** — franchise, staffing agency, joint employment and PEO arrangements change the answer.
4. **Where the work is performed** versus where the employee lives and where payroll sits.
5. **Effective dates**, including rules passed but not yet in force.
6. **City and county ordinances at each site address**, not each state.
7. **Notice, poster and language requirements** — easiest to satisfy, easiest to miss.
8. **Anything involving workers under 18**, with a specialist rather than a checklist.
9. **Recordkeeping and retention** per state, which differ from each other and from the federal position.
