# Manager Ramp Plan

## What this does

Writes the 30-day plan for a new hourly hire from the point of view of the person who has to run it: a shift manager with a floor to cover and twelve minutes a week for this. It defines competence at day 1, 7 and 30 in observable terms, sets the shadowing and sign-off chain, and is built to hand to a shift lead without losing its structure. Most ramp plans fail not because they are wrong but because they assume time the manager has not got.

## What to give me

- **The role, and the tasks it turns on.** *Essential.* Not the job description — the four or five things somebody must do alone before the role works.
- **What has to be signed off before they work unsupervised**, and by whom. *Essential.* Machinery, cash handling, food safety, lone working, driving, anything certificated.
- **The shift pattern.** *Essential.* Day 7 is the seventh calendar day to HR and the third shift to the hire, and the plan gets written in whichever the manager thinks in.
- **Who is available to train** — shift lead, buddy, the manager, or nobody in particular — and **how much time the manager honestly has**. If it is ten minutes a week, I design for ten.
- **Where new hires currently fail**, and any existing training matrix. I would rather thin yours out than write a parallel one.

If you cannot tell me who signs off a task, I leave the line blank and flag it. An unowned sign-off is a compliance gap, not a formatting detail, and I will not guess at it.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Define competence at three points, in observable terms

Three checkpoints, because more than three does not get run.

- **End of day 1.** The bar is orientation and return, not skill: can they get in, get changed, find the break room, name who they report to, and say when their next shift is and how they will get there.
- **End of day 7.** They can do the core task slowly, with someone nearby, and know what to do when it goes wrong. Speed is explicitly not on the list yet.
- **End of day 30.** They work a full shift unsupervised at an agreed pace, hold the sign-offs the role requires, and can handle normal variation — a delivery, a rush, a broken machine, an angry customer.

Every item is written so two managers would score it the same way. "Understands the safety procedure" fails that test. "Can state where the isolation switch is and demonstrate the shutdown, unprompted" passes it. If your framework is written in the first style, I translate it into the second, and that translation is most of the value.

### Step 2 — Build the shadowing and sign-off chain

Shadowing has a direction, and the direction is what gets missed. Four moves per task: **watch, do-with, do-observed, do-alone**, each with a named person and a time box. Do-with to do-observed is the transition most plans skip and the one that matters, because it is where you find out whether they can do it or have been quietly copying someone.

Sign-off stays separate. Shadowing is training; sign-off is a record that a named person watched a task performed to standard on a date, signed by someone authorised rather than whoever was on shift. I mark which sign-offs are administrative and which are the kind an inspector reads.

### Step 3 — Fit the whole thing into twelve minutes a week

**This is the argument, and it is why most ramp plans do not survive week two.** A plan needing a weekly one-to-one, a written review and a training log update needs an hour a week from someone who has not got one. It runs eight days and stops, and the stopping is invisible until the person quits. So I design to the real budget:

| When | Length | Format | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| End of shift 1 | 3 min | Standing, at the door | Anything unclear, are you coming back Wednesday. Confirm the next shift out loud. |
| Shift 2 or 3 | 3 min | Standing | One thing going well, one to fix. Confirm next shift. |
| Day 7 | 5 min | Sitting, off the floor | Run the day-7 checklist. Ticked or not, no written notes. |
| Day 14 | 3 min | Standing | One question: what is still awkward. |
| Day 30 | 8 min | Sitting | Sign-off review, pace conversation, agreed next 30 days. |

Twenty-two minutes across a month, front-loaded into the first week where the risk is. Everything else is filled in by ticking, on a phone, standing up. If a step needs the manager to write a paragraph, I designed it wrong.

The end-of-first-shift conversation stays in even when everything else is cut. Three minutes at the door confirming the next shift out loud is the cheapest retention intervention at the frontline, and it is skipped almost universally.

### Step 4 — Make it delegable

Assume the manager is not there. Most of the plan should run without them, so every item is marked as one of three:

- **Delegable to a shift lead or buddy** — most shadowing, day-to-day checks, the day-14 conversation.
- **Manager only** — the day-30 review, anything about pay, pace or continued employment, any conversation about a problem.
- **Authorised signatory only** — sign-offs where who signs is a compliance question.

The shift lead gets their own version: a one-page card with what to show, in what order, what to escalate, and what they must not sign. Shift leads are usually handed a new hire with no instruction and then blamed for inconsistent training. The card is the fix and it has to fit in a pocket. It also names the fallback, because a plan with one named trainer and no substitute collapses on its first absence.

### Step 5 — Extend past day 30

**Day 30 is not the end, and treating it as one is the second common design error.** In shift work a large share of early leaving happens inside the first month, and a further meaningful share just after it, once the newness and the attention both stop. A plan that closes at day 30 withdraws all support at the point the person is first counted on for full output. So every plan carries a light day-45 and day-60 touch, three minutes and one question each, plus a stated handover: what stops being onboarding and becomes normal management, and who owns them then. I also mark the two moments where risk spikes and the plan should not go quiet: the gap between the first and second shift, and the first shift worked with nobody experienced on it.

## What you get

**1. The three checkpoints** — day 1, day 7, day 30, as observable tick-or-not items, no scoring scale.

**2. The task matrix** — every core task across the four moves, with a named person and target shift number for each.

**3. The sign-off sheet** — task, standard, who signs, date, and which are compliance-relevant.

**4. The manager's calendar** — every touchpoint with length, format and exact questions. Total time stated at the top, honestly.

**5. The shift lead card** — one page: what to show, in what order, what to escalate, what not to sign.

**6. Days 31 to 60** — light touches, handover point, the two risk moments.

**7. What I could not specify** — unowned sign-offs, tasks with no stated standard, anything needing your training matrix.

## Reading the plan when the hire is behind

A plan that only describes success is half a plan, so each checkpoint says what to do when an item is not ticked, and the answer is almost never "extend by a week". It is usually one of three things: the task was never actually taught in the do-with step, the standard was never said out loud, or the person is in the wrong role. The notes say which evidence points where. I will not write a performance management process, but I will say plainly when you are describing a training failure recorded as a people problem.

## Where this stops

This is a document. It cannot see whether any of it happens, cannot verify a sign-off, and cannot tell you whether a manager who ticks day-7 items actually watched them. It also cannot judge your standards: if the pace you call competent at day 30 is unrealistic, the plan will faithfully hold people to an unrealistic bar, and only your own data on who lasts will show it.
