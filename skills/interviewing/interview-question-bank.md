# Interview Question Bank

## What this does

Builds the questions your managers ask, organised by role family rather than by requisition, each with the probes that rescue a one-word answer and a note on what a good answer contains. The design decision that matters: a bank is only worth having if the same questions get asked repeatedly, so the useful bank is small, and most of the work is deciding what to leave out.

## What to give me

- **Your role families**, or your job titles and I'll group them. Essential.
- **What separates a six-month hire from a three-week hire** in each family. Essential, and it is usually not skill.
- **Interview length and who runs it** — GM, shift lead, recruiter, whoever is on shift.
- **Your scorecard or competency framework**, if one exists. Questions get built to evidence criteria; without criteria I am writing conversation starters.
- **Questions you already ask**, including the ones you suspect are bad.
- **The jurisdictions you hire in**, for the what-not-to-ask list, which varies by place.

Where you have no scorecard, I'll say which questions are therefore unmoored rather than implying they score something.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Build per role family, not per requisition

A bank written per requisition produces two hundred slightly different question sets, no comparability across sites, and a maintenance job nobody does. A bank written per family produces four or five sets used thousands of times, which is the only volume at which one repays writing.

A role family is a group of jobs that fail for the same reason. Not the same tasks, the same failure mode:

- **Customer-facing hourly** — tills, counters, front of house. Turns on pressure and pace with people watching.
- **Physical and repetitive** — warehouse, production, cleaning, kitchen. Turns on stamina, tolerance of monotony, and turning up.
- **Solo and mobile** — drivers, delivery, field service, lone security. Turns on judgement without supervision.
- **Safety-critical and procedural** — plant, machinery, food safety, care. Turns on following the procedure when the shortcut is faster.
- **First-line supervisory** — shift lead, team lead. Turns on handling a person, not a task.

Four questions shared across a family beat forty specific to one requisition, because the shared four produce comparable answers at every site hiring that family. Requisition detail belongs in the job preview question.

### Step 2 — Write questions that produce evidence, not opinions

Every question has to survive one test: does the likely answer contain a fact somebody can score? So the bank is built from three kinds.

**Past behaviour, anchored to an occasion.** "Tell me about the last time you had to start work before 6am. What time did you leave the house?" The specificity is the mechanism: "have you worked early shifts?" gets a yes, while "the last time" gets a story with details in it, and details are scoreable.

**Realistic job preview.** State the genuinely hard part in one sentence, then ask what they'd do with it. This screens and it self-selects, and the self-selection is worth more than the score.

**Confirmation.** Availability, the journey, the earliest shift, put into speech rather than left as a tapped box.

Two to four per criterion, so questions can rotate. Not thirty.

### Step 3 — Design the probes, because one-word answers are the default

Untrained interviewers get monosyllabic answers and move on, and then the guide gets blamed. Frontline candidates are frequently nervous, frequently in a car park, and frequently have never been interviewed. So every question carries its probes on the same line, labelled by type so the interviewer picks rather than invents:

| Probe type | Wording | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Specificity** | "When was that? What happened next?" | A general claim with no occasion in it |
| **Action** | "What did *you* do?" | The answer describes what the team or the manager did |
| **Consequence** | "How did it turn out? What did you do differently after?" | The answer stops at the action |
| **Prompt** | "Even something small, from school or a family thing" | A genuine blank, no work history to draw on |

The prompt probe matters most in hourly hiring and is the one that gets left out. A first-jobber with nothing to say about work usually has plenty to say about turning up somewhere on time for two years, and refusing to prompt scores nervousness as absence of evidence.

Two probes maximum, then move on, or fifteen minutes becomes twenty-five.

### Step 4 — Write scoring notes, not just questions

Each question gets a note: what a strong answer contains, what a weak one contains, and the trap. The trap is the specific way this question misleads. On reliability, it is scoring confidence rather than content — "I'm never late, ever" with no example scores below the answer describing the bus and the fallback. On a job preview question, it is scoring enthusiasm.

Where you have a scorecard, these notes map onto its anchors so the two can't drift apart. Where you don't, ask me for `scorecard-designer` from this library first: questions without criteria produce interviews that feel thorough and decide nothing.

### Step 5 — Rotate without breaking comparability

Rotation is a real need. The same four questions at one site for eight months get coached, shared in group chats and posted online, and a rehearsed answer is worth nothing. Rotate within a criterion, at matched difficulty, never mid-cohort:

- Each criterion holds two to four interchangeable variants, written to the same difficulty and scored against the same anchors, so a swap does not change what the score means.
- You rotate on a schedule, quarterly is usually right, not per interviewer and not per candidate. Everybody in one hiring wave gets the same set, or you cannot compare them to each other, which was the point.
- Confirmation questions never rotate. Availability, journey and prerequisites are asked identically forever.
- A new variant arrives with its calibration answers written, or anchors drift.

Rotate more freely than that and you have abandoned structure while keeping the paperwork.

### Step 6 — Write the what-not-to-ask list with the alternative attached

A list of banned questions does not work on its own, because the manager asked for a reason and still has the underlying need. So every entry names the safe question that meets it:

| Do not ask | Ask instead |
|---|---|
| "Do you have kids?" / "Who looks after them?" | "The shifts are Thursday to Sunday, 2pm to 10pm. Can you work those?" |
| "How old are you?" | "This role requires you to be over 18. Are you?" |
| "Any health problems?" / "How many sick days last year?" | "The job involves repeated 15kg lifts and eight hours standing. Can you do that, with any adjustments you need?" |
| "Where are you from?" | "Do you have the right to work here?" |
| "Are you planning to have children?" | Nothing. There is no version of this question |
| "Is Sunday a problem for you?" | "The rota includes one weekend in three. Can you work that?" |
| "Any gaps in your work history?" | "Have you worked to a rota before? Tell me about it" |
| "Is English your first language?" | "The job needs you to read a safety notice and take a verbal instruction. Is that OK?" |
| "How would your disability affect this?" | Describe the tasks, ask if they can do them, offer the adjustments conversation |

This list prints on the guide itself, not in a policy document, because the moment a manager reaches for one of these is mid-interview.

## What you get

**1. The bank**, grouped by role family, each question mapped to the criterion it evidences.

**2. Probes**, written out, labelled by type, on the same line as the question.

**3. Scoring notes** — strong answer, weak answer, and the trap, per question.

**4. Rotation sets** — matched variants per criterion, with the rule and the schedule.

**5. The what-not-to-ask list**, with a safe alternative beside every entry.

**6. Calibration answers** — sample responses at three levels per question, for getting several interviewers onto one bar.

**7. What I could not build** — criteria your interview length cannot evidence, named rather than padded around.

## What I'll push back on

- **Brainteasers and puzzles.** "How many tennis balls fit in this room" predicts nothing in any hourly role. What it does predict is how much interview practice someone has had, which proxies for education and confidence, and it loses you candidates who conclude you are not serious.
- **Hypotheticals as the main diet.** "What would you do if a customer shouted at you?" gets the answer the candidate thinks you want, and that answer is easy to guess. Past behaviour gets a story that can be probed. I keep one hypothetical, inside the job preview, where its job is to make the candidate picture the shift rather than to score them.
- **A bank of two hundred questions.** It reads as thorough and it destroys the thing a bank exists for. If every manager picks their own six from two hundred, no two candidates were assessed on the same basis and the scores can't be pooled across sites. Twelve to twenty per family, rotated deliberately, is a bank. Two hundred is a library nobody borrows from twice.
- **"Where do you see yourself in five years?"** in a role averaging twelve months' tenure. It rewards rehearsal and says nothing about next Tuesday. Strengths and weaknesses go the same way: universally prepared, universally unscoreable.
- **Questions written to no criterion.** If nobody can say which score a question moves, it is a conversation, and fifteen minutes can't afford one.

## Where this stops

This writes questions. It cannot tell you whether they predict anything at your sites, which would take scoring several hundred candidates against 90-day retention with data I don't have. It also cannot see what managers actually ask once the door closes, and the gap between the bank and the room is the largest single source of inconsistency. A calibration session catches more of that than any amount of rewriting.
