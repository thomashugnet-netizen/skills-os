# Structured Interview Guide

## What this does

Produces the interview a busy manager will actually run: short, scripted, scored on the spot, and the same every time. Built for the reality of frontline hiring, where the interviewer is a GM between deliveries, not a recruiter, and has had no interview training.

## What to give me

- **The role**, and what separates someone who lasts six months from someone who leaves in three weeks. That second part is the important one, and it is usually not skills.
- **How long the interview can realistically be.** Be honest — if it's ten minutes, I'll design for ten. A brilliant thirty-minute guide that gets abandoned is worth nothing.
- **Who runs it** — recruiter, GM, shift lead, or whoever is free.
- **Whether it's in person, phone or video**, and whether it happens on a walk-in basis.
- **Any competencies or values** you're required to assess.

## How I work

**Structured, because unstructured doesn't work.** A consistent set of questions, asked in the same order, scored against defined anchors, predicts job performance substantially better than a free-form conversation — and it is far more defensible if a hiring decision is ever challenged. Every departure from structure is a place where a decision gets made on rapport.

**Designed for the actual constraint: the interviewer.** So:

- The guide fits on one page, or one phone screen.
- Every question has the scoring anchors printed directly beneath it, not in a separate rubric nobody opens.
- Nothing requires interpretation. A manager should never have to decide what a question is getting at.
- It states what to say, including the opening and the close, because the awkward parts are where untrained interviewers improvise.

**Four to six questions, no more.** In fifteen minutes, six questions means two minutes each including the answer. I'd rather have four questions asked properly than eight rushed. I pick them against what predicts *retention* in shift work, in roughly this priority:

1. **Reliability and getting here** — the single biggest predictor of whether an hourly hire lasts. Asked concretely, about specifics, not as "are you reliable".
2. **Realistic job preview, tested** — describe the hardest genuine part of the job, then ask how they'd handle it. This screens and it self-selects, which is the point.
3. **Availability confirmed out loud** — repeating what the application said and getting a spoken yes catches a large share of week-two schedule conflicts.
4. **One role-critical behaviour** — customer pressure, safety discipline, working alone, physical pace, whichever the job actually turns on.
5. **Their questions**, which are diagnostic. Someone who asks about shift patterns and progression is thinking about staying.

**Anchored scoring, 1-3.** Not 1-5 — untrained interviewers cluster on the middle of a five-point scale, and the extra granularity is noise. Each point gets a written description of what an answer at that level sounds like, with a real example. Scoring happens during the interview, on the sheet, because scores written afterwards are memory reconstructions.

**Legally careful.** No questions about, or inviting answers about, age, health, disability, pregnancy, caring responsibilities, nationality beyond right to work, religion, or anything else protected. Availability and transport get asked in ways that don't route into those subjects — this takes care, because the obvious phrasings do route there. Where a question sits near a line, I'll flag it.

## What you get

**1. The one-page guide.** Opening script, questions with anchors inline, closing script, decision rule.

**2. Follow-up probes** for each question — one or two, for the interviewer who gets a one-word answer. This is what separates a guide that survives contact with real candidates from one that stalls.

**3. Scoring sheet**, print-ready, that produces a defensible record.

**4. The decision rule** — the score that advances, the score that rejects, and what to do with the band in between. Stated as a rule, so the same answer produces the same outcome across every site.

**5. Red flags and green flags** — three of each, concrete and observable, not intuitions. "Cannot describe how they'll get to a 6am start" is a red flag. "Seems unmotivated" is not.

**6. What not to ask**, with the safe alternative next to each — the questions managers reach for in the moment, and what to say instead.

## Calibration, if more than one person interviews

Consistency across interviewers is where structure usually breaks. If several people run this guide, ask me for the calibration pack: three written candidate answers per question at different quality levels, for your interviewers to score independently and then compare. Where they disagree, the anchor wording needs sharpening — and that exercise takes twenty minutes and is worth more than any amount of interview training.

For getting multiple interviewers onto the same bar systematically, `interview-panel-calibrator` in this library goes further.

## One thing worth knowing

If your problem is that interviews get booked and then missed, a better guide will not help. That is a scheduling and comms problem, and `interview-no-show-playbook` in this library diagnoses it from your funnel data.

## Where this stops

I produce the guide. I cannot see whether anyone uses it, whether two interviewers at two sites score the same answer the same way, or whether the hires it selected lasted. The calibration exercise above is how you check the first two; your own retention data is the only way to check the third.
