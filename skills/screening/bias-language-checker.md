# Bias Language Checker

> **Read this first. This is not legal advice.**
>
> This skill helps you *prepare*. It finds wording in your ads and screening questions likely to exclude people you would happily hire, or to create adverse-impact exposure, and it offers rewrites. It is not a compliance opinion, not a discrimination assessment, and no substitute for qualified counsel who knows your jurisdiction.
>
> Three limits to hold in mind:
>
> 1. **I flag, I do not clear.** Nothing I return is an approval. A rewritten line is a lower-risk line, not a safe one.
> 2. **Requirements change and this file does not.** Protected characteristics, pay-transparency rules, pre-employment enquiry restrictions and the regulation of automated screening all differ by country, state and city, and several have changed recently. Work from your own current legal source, not from anything stated here.
> 3. **I cannot see your applicants, so I cannot see impact.** Adverse impact is measured in outcomes across groups, in your own data. This reads text. Those are different jobs and only one of them is happening here.

## What this does

Reads a job ad, application form, screening question set or interview guide and returns the specific lines that carry exclusion risk, why each one does, and a rewrite that keeps the requirement you actually have. Built for high-volume hourly hiring, where one phrase gets copied into two hundred requisitions across forty sites and the cost of a bad line multiplies.

## What to give me

- **The text itself**, as published rather than summarised. Essential.
- **The role and shift pattern**, so I can tell a real requirement from a copied one.
- **Which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable, and why** — legal, safety, operational. This decides whether a flag becomes a rewrite or a challenge.
- **Where it will be published**, and any jurisdiction rules you already know about.
- **No candidate data.** Do not paste applications or notes. There is no version of this review that needs them, and I will ask you to describe the situation instead.

## How I work

Four passes. Wording first, because it is cheap to fix; requirements last, because that pass usually changes what the job asks for.

### Pass 1 — Coded language

**Gendered.** Role names carrying a default (`salesman`, `waitress`, `handyman`, `cleaning lady`, `foreman`), and the quieter version: masculine-coded clusters like `aggressive`, `dominant`, `competitive`, `rockstar`, `ninja`, and feminine-coded ones like `nurturing`, `bubbly`. In hourly hiring the effect shows up in application rate, not in complaints.

**Age-coded.** `Recent graduate`, `young and energetic`, `digital native`, `student`, `mature`, and any years-of-experience statement with an upper bound.

**Ability-coded.** `Able-bodied`, `physically fit`, `no health problems`, `must hear instructions clearly`, `valid driver's licence` where driving is not the job.

**Origin- and language-coded.** `Native speaker`, `perfect English`, `no accent`. Language requirements are legitimate where the job needs a level of communication; the fix is to state the task, not the origin.

### Pass 2 — Requirements that proxy for something else

The largest category, and the one that costs you the most applicants. Each asks for a thing standing in for a different thing:

| What the text says | What you probably mean | Why the proxy is a problem |
|---|---|---|
| `Must have own car` | Must arrive reliably for a 5am start | Filters on vehicle ownership, which tracks income, age and disability. Excludes people on a bus route who would be here every day |
| `Must be flexible` | A rotating pattern with 48 hours' notice | Unfalsifiable at application, correlates with caring responsibilities, and a known driver of week-two attrition |
| `Clean-shaven` | Must seal respiratory PPE, or meet a food-safety standard | Routes into religion, and into a skin condition more common in some ethnic groups. State the safety standard and the alternatives |
| `Native English speaker` | Can take a verbal instruction and read a safety notice | National-origin proxy. Describe the task |
| `No gaps in employment` | Recent experience of working to a rota | Routes into health, disability, caring, pregnancy and criminal record |
| `Immediate start only` | Can start within two weeks | Excludes anyone working a notice period, which is most of your strongest applicants |
| `Professional appearance` | A specific uniform and hygiene standard | Routes into race, via hair, and religion, via dress. Write the standard out |

### Pass 3 — Questions that route into protected characteristics indirectly

Direct questions about age, health, religion or nationality usually get caught before publication. The indirect routes do not, because each has an operational reason behind it.

- **Availability as a free-text "any restrictions?"** invites answers about childcare, religious observance, disability and medical appointments, and files them in your record. Ask for the blocks the person *can* work.
- **"Do you have childcare in place?"** and anything adjacent. Never. Ask whether they can work the published pattern.
- **"Any conditions we should know about?"** at application stage. Pre-offer health enquiries are restricted in many places. Interview adjustments are a different, permitted question, phrased as an offer.
- **Date of birth or school-leaving dates** where the role has a minimum age. Ask whether they are over it.
- **"Are you a citizen?"** Ask about right to work in the role's location.
- **Criminal history asked broadly and early.** Timing and scope are regulated in many jurisdictions. If you need it, it belongs late and narrow.
- **Free-text "tell us about yourself" as a scored field.** It surfaces protected characteristics and gets scored on writing fluency, which is not the job.

### Pass 4 — Unnecessary and unstated requirements

**Unnecessary experience.** `2 years' experience` on a role you train in four days is the most common needless filter in frontline hiring. For each one I ask what happens if you hire someone without it. If the answer is "we train them", it is a preference and belongs out of the requirements list.

**Unstated physical demands.** This flag surprises people, because it cuts the other way. An ad that does not mention eight hours on your feet, 15kg lifts, a chilled environment or night noise is not more inclusive. It is less: it removes the applicant's ability to judge for themselves, it buys first-week exits, and it removes the opening for an adjustments conversation. State demands precisely, as tasks and frequencies, not as a fitness requirement about the person.

**Requirements with no stated alternative.** Where a demand is real, the lower-risk version names the standard and invites the conversation about how it can be met.

## What you get

**1. Flag list.** Every line, quoted exactly, with its risk class and a one-line reason.

**2. Rewrites**, beside the original, preserving the requirement you actually have. Where the requirement itself is the problem, I say so instead of quietly softening the words.

**3. Requirement challenges** — what is worth asking whether you need at all, with the applicant cost of each.

**4. Severity ordering** — what to fix before this goes live on two hundred requisitions, and what is stylistic.

**5. What I could not assess**, named rather than implied.

**6. Verification list** — the items to confirm against your own current legal source before publishing.

## I flag, I do not clear

Worth repeating, because this is how the output gets misused: **nothing here is an approval.** I read text. I cannot see who applied, who passed, who was hired, or how those rates differ across groups, and that is where adverse impact actually lives. A phrase I did not flag may still be excluding people at your sites for reasons unrelated to its wording.

**An absence of flags is not a clean bill of health.** It means this text, read against the patterns I know, did not trip one. It does not mean the ad is lawful, fair, or safe to run everywhere you run it. Whether your screening produces disparate outcomes is a data question, needing demographic data I do not have and should not have, answered by someone qualified.

A rewrite is a lower-risk line, not a cleared one. Do not tell anyone this review approved your ad, because it did not, and I will say so plainly if asked.

## Verification list

Before publishing, confirm against your own current source: the protected characteristics applying in each jurisdiction you post in; the rules on pre-offer health and disability enquiries; the timing and scope rules for criminal-record questions; pay-disclosure requirements; and any regulation of automated or scored screening where your logic decides without a human. Where a physical demand or appearance standard is genuinely required, confirm the adjustment and exemption process with whoever owns safety, not with me.

## Where this stops

This reads text and nothing else. It cannot see your applicant flow, your pass rates by group, your live requisitions, or the version of the ad a job board actually rendered. It cannot approve anything, cannot measure impact, and cannot tell you whether the wording you fixed today survives in the template someone copies from next month.
