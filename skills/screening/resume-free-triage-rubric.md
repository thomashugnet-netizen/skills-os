# Resume-Free Triage Rubric

## What this does

Builds the rubric your team uses to decide who gets a call, for roles where most applicants attach nothing and the rest attach a document written for a different job. It replaces "read the CV" with a small set of scored signals that predict starting and staying, and it is built so the two signals that feel most decisive — where someone lives, when they can work — score rather than reject.

## What to give me

- **The role, the site, and the shifts you are short of.** Essential. Availability is scored against the holes in your rota, not against "any hours".
- **What your application form currently asks.** Essential. The rubric can only score what you collect, and the usual finding is that you collect the wrong five things.
- **Applications a week, and calls your team can make a week.** Essential. This ratio sets every threshold.
- **Requirements that are genuinely hard** — minimum age for the role, a licence the law requires, right to work.
- **Transport reality at the site**: bus routes and first arrivals, parking, whether nothing runs to the estate at 5am.
- **Retention data**, if you have any. Even a rough sense of which hires last beats my priors.

Where you can't tell me one of these, I'll name the part of the rubric that is then guesswork rather than filling it in quietly.

## How I work

### Step 1 — Establish what you are triaging for

Triage is not ranking. It answers one question: who gets a call today, given that eight hundred people applied and your team can make forty calls. A rubric that returns two hundred "call now" applicants has not triaged anything, and the queue then gets worked in whatever order the list happens to sort, which is worse than random.

If volume and capacity cannot be reconciled by any rubric — eighty starts needed a week, two hundred applications — that is the finding, and no scoring design fixes it.

### Step 2 — Score the signals that survive having no work history

With no resume, five things carry most of the predictive weight. In order:

| Signal | How it's scored | Why it predicts |
|---|---|---|
| **Shift overlap** | Blocks they can work, as a percentage of your unfilled hours | Strongest single signal. A mismatch here produces a hire who leaves in week three |
| **Start latency** | Days until they can start; notice owed | Monday beats five weeks away, because five weeks is long enough for three other employers to hire them |
| **Travel realism** | Journey feasibility for the earliest shift, in bands | A plan that works at 5am beats a short distance with no plan. See Step 3 |
| **Reliability, asked concretely** | "Have you worked shifts starting before 7am?" "What did you do when the bus was late?" | One scored answer here beats any inference from a document |
| **Certifications you need** | Forklift, food hygiene, SIA, CPC, first aid | A bonus where they save training time; a knockout only where the law makes them one |

Job titles, employer names and years of experience are weak positive evidence and nothing more. Having done the job makes someone easier to train. That is all it tells you.

### Step 3 — Weight travel and availability without turning them into knockouts

These two are where resume-free triage goes wrong, and it goes wrong the same way each time: they get built as filters, because filters are easy to configure.

**Travel.** Straight-line distance is a bad proxy for arriving at a 6am start. Ten kilometres with a car and free parking is twelve minutes. Four kilometres across a river with one bus that starts at 06:40 is impossible. A radius rule scores those the wrong way round, and it does so systematically: the people it excludes are disproportionately those without a car, which tracks income, age, disability and, at many sites, ethnicity. That is an adverse-impact exposure and a supply problem at once, and it is invisible in your funnel because rejected applicants generate no further data.

So travel scores on journey feasibility for the earliest shift, in bands, with the applicant's own stated plan as an input. A plan naming the route and the time scores above a short distance with no plan.

**Availability.** Scored as overlap, never as a gate. A rota is a set of holes rather than one requirement, and someone who cannot do Saturday nights may be exactly who you need for Tuesday mornings. Where overlap is partial, the rubric routes: another site, another pattern, or a hold list for the next rota change. Anywhere but reject.

The rule I apply throughout: a signal becomes a knockout only if the answer means you cannot legally or safely employ this person for any shift at any of your sites. Otherwise it is points.

### Step 4 — Handle gaps and first jobs deliberately

Two groups get quietly filtered out by anything resume-shaped, and both contain people who stay longer than average once hired.

**Genuine gaps.** Caring, illness, immigration, parental leave, a collapsed sector. A gap is not a signal about work quality, and asking someone to explain one at triage routes straight into health, disability, caring responsibilities and pregnancy, then puts the answer in your record. So the rubric neither asks about gaps nor scores them. If recent continuity genuinely matters, score what you need: have they worked to a rota, ever.

**First jobs.** No work history is not a low score, it is one absent signal. I build the rubric so an applicant can clear the call threshold on availability, travel realism, start latency and one reliability answer. School, college, sport, volunteering and caring all demonstrate turning up somewhere on time repeatedly, and at volume this group is often the difference between filling the rota and not.

**Re-applicants** get a rule of their own: a rejection for a rota that no longer exists is not evidence about the person, and a third application shows motivation rather than desperation.

### Step 5 — Set bands, and say what happens in each

Three bands, each with an action and a deadline: call today, call if the top band runs dry this week, hold with a stated reason. Plus a knockout list short enough to read aloud.

I also write what a reviewer does with a blank field, because at frontline volume 10-20% of forms have one. A missing answer is a question for the call, not a zero. Scoring it as a zero silently rejects everyone who abandoned a slow form on a phone.

## What you get

**1. The rubric** — signals, weights, points, on one page.

**2. Collection gaps** — fields to add to your form, ranked by predictive value per extra question, because every question costs applications on mobile.

**3. Band thresholds** tuned to your call capacity, with predicted volume in each and how it moves if you shift one.

**4. The knockout list**, with the test each one passed to get there, plus the routing rules for partial availability and travel-constrained applicants.

**5. Edge-case rules** — gaps, first jobs, re-applicants, blank fields.

**6. Adverse-impact notes** — signals that could proxy for a protected characteristic, and how the wording reduces it. A flag, not a clearance: anything automated should be reviewed by someone qualified with your own legal position in front of them.

## What I'll push back on

- **Treating no resume as no experience.** Most people who have held three shift jobs have never written a CV, because nobody asked them for one. An empty attachment field tells you about your form, not your applicant.
- **A commute radius.** It reads as objective and it is easy to configure, and at most sites it excludes people twenty minutes away on a bus while admitting people eight kilometres away with no way to arrive before 7am.
- **Availability as a hard filter.** The most common way frontline employers manufacture their own shortage.
- **Scoring stability from job tenure.** Two years at one employer and four jobs in two years often reflect sector rather than person. Warehouse and hospitality churn is structural.
- **Twelve fields so the rubric has more inputs.** Each question costs completed applications. I'll tell you which four are worth their abandonment cost.

## Where this stops

This designs the rubric. It cannot tell you how it scores your actual applicants, and it cannot see the people your current rules already rejected, which is exactly the group who would tell you whether the weights are right. It also cannot verify any travel claim: the journey gets confirmed out loud on the phone screen, not inferred from an address.
