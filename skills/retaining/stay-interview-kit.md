# Stay Interview Kit

## What this does

An exit interview tells you why someone left, three weeks too late, from a person with no reason to be careful and no reason to be honest. A stay interview asks the same questions of someone who is still on shift and can still be kept. This builds the questions, decides who asks them and when, and — the part most stay-interview programmes skip — sets out what happens to the answers. In frontline work the fixable causes are boringly consistent: the shift, the journey, the hours, the supervisor. Those are the things I aim the questions at.

## What to give me

- **The role and the site type**, and roughly how long people last. If you know your 30 and 90-day attrition, tell me — the cadence hangs off it. *Essential.*
- **What you can actually change.** Shifts, swaps, start times, break rota, transport support, equipment, pay bands, progression. Be honest about the fixed ones. *Essential — this determines which questions I include at all.*
- **Who is available to run these**, and how much of their shift you can protect for it.
- **Your span of control** — how many direct reports a supervisor has. At 40:1 this becomes a different exercise than at 8:1.
- **Whether the same supervisor handles scheduling.** It changes who should ask.
- **Anything already running** — surveys, pulse tools, skip-levels — so I don't stack another conversation on top of one nobody answers.

## How I work

**I build a conversation, not a survey.** This is the first thing I will push back on. Stay interviews get turned into a form because a form scales, and the moment it becomes a form it stops working: the answers get shorter, the specifics vanish, and "how satisfied are you with your schedule, 1 to 5" produces a 3 from someone whose real answer is that the 5:45 bus doesn't run on Sundays. The value is entirely in the second and third follow-up question, which a form cannot ask. If you need a number across 40 sites, run a survey and call it a survey. Do not call it a stay interview and expect it to keep anyone.

**Six to eight questions, ten to fifteen minutes, on shift, seated.** Not in the manager's office with the door shut, which reads as a disciplinary. Not at the end of a shift, when the person wants to go home.

**Who asks.** The default assumption is the direct supervisor, and the default assumption is often wrong. In hourly work the supervisor is one of the most common reasons people leave, and asking someone to disclose that to the person it is about produces a polite nothing. I'll pick from:

- **Direct supervisor** — fine where the relationship is the strength, and where they control the schedule.
- **Second-line manager or a neighbouring site lead** — the usual right answer for the day 30 and day 90 conversations.
- **A trained peer or shift lead** — highest disclosure rate, lowest authority to fix anything, so only useful if the escalation path is real.
- **HR or ops, remotely** — works for multi-site estates, loses the on-shift informality.

Where I recommend someone other than the supervisor, I'll say why in one line, and I'll give you a version the supervisor *can* run, because in a 40-site estate you will not staff it any other way for everyone.

**The questions aim at what you can fix.** Open questions about engagement produce answers you cannot act on. So the core set covers the journey in (route, cost, reliability, what time they actually leave home), the schedule (which shifts they'd take more of, which they dread, what they had to turn down), the first two weeks (what surprised them, what they were not told), the equipment and the small daily friction, who they'd go to with a problem, and one direct question on what would make them look elsewhere. I'll include a version of the "if you left, what would be the reason" question, because it gets a straighter answer than asking whether they intend to leave.

**I will cut any question you cannot act on.** If shift patterns are fixed by a central rota nobody at the site can change, asking someone which shift they'd prefer and then doing nothing is worse than never asking. That is not squeamishness — it is the main way these programmes fail. Asking creates an expectation of a response, and an unanswered expectation converts a mildly dissatisfied worker into one who now knows nothing will change. The rule I apply: every question either routes to something changeable, or routes to something you will honestly explain. Nothing else goes in.

**Timed at the risk points, not annually.** An annual cycle is designed around a performance calendar for salaried staff. Hourly attrition is front-loaded, so the conversations sit where the leaving happens: end of week one, around day 30, around day 90, then twice a year. Plus event-triggered ones — after a shift-pattern change, after a site or supervisor change, after a first missed shift, and after a peak period ends. I'll size the total load against your span of control and tell you if it doesn't fit; if it doesn't, I'd rather cut the six-month cycle than the day 30.

## What you get

**1. The question set**, in ask-order, with the follow-up probes underneath each one. The probes are the part that makes it work.

**2. Interviewer notes** — how to open it so it doesn't sound like a warning, what to do with silence, and what not to promise.

**3. The cadence**, with the fixed points and the event triggers, and who runs each.

**4. A capture template** — themes and specifics, not scores. Two fields per answer: what they said, and what was requested.

**5. Escalation and action rules.** What the interviewer can fix on the spot, what goes to the site lead this week, what goes to ops, and what must be answered even when the answer is no.

**6. Close-the-loop scripts** — what to say back to the person within seven days, for each of: fixed it, working on it, can't do it and here's why.

**7. What to ask that you legally should not**, with safe alternatives — the health, caring-responsibility and immigration-adjacent phrasings that the obvious wording routes straight into.

## Reading the answers without over-reading them

One person's account of their site is one person's account. The pattern only appears at ten or fifteen conversations, so resist rewriting a rota off a single strongly-put complaint. Two things are worth acting on immediately from a single conversation: anything safety-related, and anything that names a specific broken mechanism — a swap request that went unanswered for three weeks, a rota published on a Saturday for a Monday. Those are facts, not sentiments, and you can check them.

Also watch what the answers are *not*. If nobody mentions their supervisor in either direction, and the supervisor ran the interviews, you have learned nothing about the supervisor.

## Where this stops

This produces the questions, the schedule and the follow-up rules. It cannot run the conversations, cannot see who is at day 30 today, and cannot make a supervisor hold a conversation they don't want to hold. It will not tell you whether the commitments made in these interviews were ever honoured — that is a promise-tracking problem, and only your own records can answer it.
