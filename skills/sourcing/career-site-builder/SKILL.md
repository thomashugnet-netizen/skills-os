# Career Site Builder

## What this does

Give me an employer's name and website and I build its careers site: home page, career areas, FAQ, hiring process, one page per job, one page per location, with a job search that works on a phone. Or give me the careers site you already have, and I tell you what it is costing you before I rebuild it.

The insight that makes this more than a pretty page: a careers site is judged by machines before it is judged by candidates. A search engine decides whether your jobs appear in its job listings, an AI assistant decides whether to mention you when someone asks who is hiring nearby, and both read the HTML, not the design. So the parts that decide whether anyone finds the site are generated by code from your jobs file, and a checker verifies them before you see anything.

## What to give me

Essential, one of:

- **An employer name and website**, to build a new site. I research the rest.
- **A careers site URL**, to audit first. I try to download the page itself. If my environment cannot reach it, I ask you to save it from your browser (File, Save Page As, HTML only) and upload it, and I start the build while you do.
- **Nothing at all.** Say "show me a demo" and I build a complete site for a fictional employer in about two minutes, so you can see what you would get.

Better, if you have them:

- **A jobs export** from your applicant tracking system, or a spreadsheet, one row per open job. The columns are in `references/seo-ai-spec.md`; title, city, pay, schedule and apply link are the ones that matter most. Without it I build the site with sample jobs clearly labelled as samples.
- **Brand assets**: logo, colour codes, any photos of real employees at work.
- **The facts research cannot find**: how fast you reply to applicants, when benefits start, the minimum age, real promotion timings.
- **Where the site will live**, so canonical URLs and the sitemap point to the right place.

## How I work

### Step 0: The demo, if you asked for one

I run `python3 scripts/build_site.py --site northline-demo --demo` from this skill's folder, writing into the folder where I put files for you to download, then `scripts/check_site.py --site northline-demo`. You get the styled preview, the full site as a zip and the check report within a couple of minutes. Then I offer to do the same for your company.

### Step 1: Audit, if there is a site already

I run `scripts/check_site.py --page` on your current careers page and show you the count: how many of the checks it passes, area by area, and the blocking failures first. The most common one is a site rendered entirely by JavaScript, which many AI crawlers see as an empty page. This comes first because it is the baseline the new site has to beat.

### Step 2: Research

Your public brand, footprint, job categories, benefits, culture and current candidate experience, from your own sources first. If I have no web search in this conversation, I ask you to paste your About and Benefits pages and work from those. Every fact I use is either sourced or wrapped in a confirmation mark. I invent no statistics, benefits, quotes or awards.

### Step 3: One screen of direction

The site map, the home page story section by section, the colours I took from your brand, the assumptions I made, and at most three questions that research could not answer. Then I keep going unless you tell me to stop. If you would rather approve the direction before I build, say "review first" and I wait.

### Step 4: Build

I start from the tested starter in `references/starter/`: a design system, a home page, a job search, a FAQ, a hiring process page and a career-area template. I set the brand tokens in `css/tokens.css`, rewrite every page for your employer following `references/brief.md`, and add one page per career area from `references/talent-segments.md`. Then `scripts/build_site.py` generates the job pages with JobPosting markup, the location pages, the job list in plain HTML, the FAQ markup, the sitemap, robots.txt and llms.txt. The hand-written pages stay hand-written; the script only fills the parts it owns.

### Step 5: Check, fix, check again

`scripts/check_site.py --site` runs every check in `references/rubric.md`. I fix what it finds and run it again. I do not hand over a site with a blocking finding.

If I cannot run code in this conversation, I still write the pages from the starter, but I tell you plainly that the job pages, sitemap and checks were not generated, and how to turn code execution on so they can be.

## What I'll push back on

- **Pay left off job pages.** It is the first thing a frontline candidate looks for, the field search engines reward, and in a growing number of places it is required. A job without pay says so on the page and gets flagged.
- **A JavaScript-only site.** If your current site is a single-page app, assistants may see nothing on it. Every job here is in the HTML.
- **Stock photography presented as employees.** I leave labelled placeholders that say what to shoot instead.
- **Invented testimonials.** Placeholders until real people agree to be quoted.
- **Slogans.** "We're a family" and "competitive pay" are flagged by the checker. Specific beats warm.
- **Making candidates create an account before they apply.** It loses more applicants than any design choice can win back.

## Being found by search engines and AI assistants

This is the part most careers sites get wrong and nobody notices, because the site looks fine. One page per job with complete JobPosting markup, one page per location people actually search for, titles that lead with the job and end with the brand, a sitemap, FAQ markup generated from the real questions on the page, and an llms.txt summary of pages, jobs and pay. `references/seo-ai-spec.md` explains each one and what it cannot do: nothing here guarantees a ranking, it removes the reasons to be left out.

## What it costs you to run

Without it, a custom careers site is a project: a brief, an agency or contractor, a few weeks of rounds, and a fee in the thousands. It is usually delivered as a design that is then wired to the applicant tracking system separately, which is where the job markup and location pages get lost.

After launch, the cost is the jobs. Sixty open roles across forty sites change every week. Each change means a job page added, one removed, a pay figure updated, and the sitemap and llms.txt kept in step. Rebuilding from a fresh export takes one command here, but someone has to export, rebuild and upload, every time a job opens or closes, or the site starts advertising jobs that are filled.

## Where this stops

I build and check static files. I do not host the site, connect it to your applicant tracking system live, or keep it current when jobs change; each update is a new export and a rebuild. I cannot confirm that a fact is true, only that it is marked, and I cannot judge whether your pay is competitive for an area. Pay-transparency rules are described in shape in `references/pay-transparency.md`, which is not legal advice and ends with what to verify for each location before launch.

## Verifying this yourself

Run `python3 scripts/check_site.py --test`. It builds the demo site from the starter and the sample jobs, asserts that every check passes and that every job has a page, its markup and a link from the search page, then breaks the site twenty-six ways, one at a time: a missing viewport, a JobPosting with no date, a JavaScript-only home page, a low-contrast brand colour, an award nobody marked for confirmation, an orphaned job page, and more. Each one must be caught for its named reason and nothing else, which is the half that decides whether a checker is worth keeping.

It caught a real defect while being written: job page titles built as "role in city | brand careers" ran past seventy characters, so search results would have cut off the brand and the city with it. Titles now drop the brand first.
